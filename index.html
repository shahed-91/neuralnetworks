How does something learn?

Remember learning to ride a bike? At first you wobbled and fell. Nobody handed you a rulebook. But after enough tries, your body just knew what to do.

So what changed? The answer is hiding inside your head, in a tiny cell called a neuron. Let's meet one, and then teach a computer the same trick.

Let's look inside your head

A tiny messenger in your head

Imagine a kid in a huge classroom. Friends whisper messages from every side. If enough whispers say "yes", the kid jumps up and shouts a message down the hall to the next kid.

That kid is a neuron. You have about 86 billion of them, whispering and shouting to each other right now. Poke each part of the drawing to see who does what.

Can a computer do the same trick?

Scientists asked: what if we keep only the big idea? A neuron listens to lots of messages, decides some matter more than others, adds them up, and gives one answer. That is simple enough to write as numbers. Drag the slider and watch the living cell turn into a maths machine.

The grown-up word: the maths version is called an artificial neuron.

Should I go to the park?

Let's build a neuron that makes a choice you make every day. It gets a few clues, decides how much each one matters, and gives an answer.

  1. The clues. Is it sunny? Are my friends going? Each clue is a number from 0 (no) to 1 (yes).The grown-up word: inputsWhere it stops: a real neuron gets chemical signals, not tidy numbers.
  2. How much each clue matters. Maybe sunshine matters a lot to you, and friends matter even more. A clue you dislike can even count against going.The grown-up word: weightsWhere it stops: in a brain, connections strengthen by growing and chemistry. A computer just changes a number.
  3. How much you already want to go. Some days you would go to the park no matter what.The grown-up word: biasWhere it stops: it is a rough stand-in for how ready a real cell is to fire.
  4. The decider. Multiply each clue by how much it matters, add them up, add your head start. Then turn that total into an answer between 0 (no) and 1 (yes), like a dimmer instead of a switch.The grown-up words: weighted sum and activation functionWhere it stops: real neurons fire spikes over time, not a smooth dimmer.
  5. The answer. One number that gets passed on.The grown-up word: output
