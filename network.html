Inside the artificial mind

One neuron is one kid making one choice. A network is a whole class passing notes, with each row of kids working on a different part of the puzzle.

A class that tells cats from dogs

Row one gets clues: are the ears pointy, is the snout long, are there whiskers? Row two, the thinkers, each look for a pattern in those clues. Row three gives the answer: cat or dog? Slide the clues and watch the notes pass forward.

The grown-up words: input layer, hidden layer (hidden because nobody sees what it is thinking) and output layer. Passing notes forward is called forward propagation.

How one thinker decides

Each kid in the middle row does the same little job. Every clue gets a vote, but some votes are heavier than others. Add up the votes, add a head start, and you get one number. Then the kid squashes that number into an answer from 0 to 1: how excited am I?

x
an input, the number arriving from the layer before
w
a weight, how strongly that input counts
b
the bias, a fixed nudge up or down
z
the raw total before squashing

z = wx + b

With three clues it is the same idea, just longer: z = w1x1 + w2x2 + w3x3 + b. The squashing step is the activation function. Without it, a network could only give straight-line answers. With it, the network can learn curvy, tricky ones.

Choose the hidden-layer activation

The horizontal axis is z, the vertical axis is the neuron's output. Changing it above changes the network's behaviour. The output layer always uses sigmoid.

Guessing, then getting better

Making a prediction means using what the neuron knows right now. Learning is different: it guesses, finds out how wrong it was, and adjusts, like playing hot and cold.

Here is a secret rule game. The dots are examples: put in 1 and the answer is about 2, put in 2 and it is about 4. The neuron has to work out the rule. Press train and watch the line chase the dots.

The grown-up words: how wrong the neuron is, is the loss. Nudging the weight and bias to shrink it is gradient descent.

What networks do well, and what they do not

Where they help

  • Recognising images, such as faces and medical scans.
  • Turning speech into text and one language into another.
  • Recommending music, spotting fraud, forecasting weather.
  • Generating text and pictures after training on vast examples.

Where they fall short

  • They learn from examples, so biased or poor data gives biased or poor results.
  • They can be confidently wrong on inputs unlike anything they saw in training.
  • It is often hard to explain why a network gave a particular answer.
  • They find patterns. They do not understand meaning the way a person does.
